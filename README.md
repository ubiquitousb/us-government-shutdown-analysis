# Analysis: Government Shutdowns and Election Outcomes

This repository contains a data analysis project investigating a real-world question: **Do U.S. federal government shutdowns negatively affect the party in power during the next election cycle?**

To answer this, my project uses public data to test a hypothesis using permutation testing and estimates uncertainty using bootstrapping.

---

## 📊 The Data

This analysis required sourcing, cleaning, and merging three distinct types of public datasets. The specific links to all sources and the detailed cleaning process are documented in the Jupyter Notebook.

* **1. Federal Funding Gaps:** A list of all funding gaps, their start dates, and durations.
    * **Source:** Compiled from **Congressional Research Service (CRS)** report RS20348, "Federal Funding Gaps: A Brief Overview."

* **2. Election Results:** A high-granularity dataset of U.S. House election outcomes from 1976 to the present.
    * **Source:** The **MIT Election Data and Science Lab (MEDSL)** dataset "U.S. House 1976–2024" (doi:10.7910/DVN/IG0UN2), hosted on the Harvard Dataverse.

* **3. Party in Power:** A lookup table, compiled from multiple sources, to determine which party held the Presidency, House, and Senate for each election cycle.
    * **Source (House):** Official tables from the **U.S. House of Representatives History, Art & Archives**.
    * **Source (Senate):** Official data from the **U.S. Senate History Office**.
    * **Source (President):** State of Ohio table of U.S. Presidents and Vice Presidents, including political party.

A key finding during data preparation was the distinction between "funding gaps" (pre-1980) and "shutdowns" (post-1980). 
Before 1980, agencies largely continued to operate during a gap. After a 1980 legal opinion, gaps led to the furlough of 
non-essential workers. Because this project measures the political effect, this analysis operationalizes "shutdowns" as 
the post-1980 events where the public was directly impacted.

After merging and cleaning, each row in the data represents a U.S. House district-level election (e.g., "MN-04 in 2018"). The dataset includes all 435 districts across multiple election cycles, providing thousands of individual observations for the analysis.

## 💡 Hypothesis

The central question is formalized into a testable hypothesis.

* **Test Statistic:** The **difference in means** of the `vote_share_swing` for the President's party.
* **Null Hypothesis:** There is no significant difference in the mean vote share swing for the President's party between election cycles preceded by a government shutdown and those that were not. Any observed difference is due to random chance.
* **Alternative Hypothesis:** Election cycles preceded by a government shutdown will show a **lower** mean vote share swing for the President's party, indicating a political "cost" for the shutdown.

---

## ⚙️ Analysis & Methodology

The analysis is conducted in the `shutdown_analysis.ipynb` notebook and follows these steps:

1.  **Data Sourcing and Preparation:** The three datasets (Shutdowns, Election Results, Party Control) are loaded, cleaned, and merged into a single analytical dataframe.
2.  **Descriptive Statistics:** Initial exploration and visualization are performed to understand the data's structure and distributions.
3.  **Permutation Test (Hypothesis Test):** A permutation test is run to evaluate the null hypothesis. The `vote_share_swing` is compared between the two groups ("shutdown cycle" vs. "no shutdown cycle") by shuffling the group labels 10,000 times to build a reference distribution of the test statistic.
4.  **Bootstrap Uncertainty:** Uncertainty is estimated for a key metric where the sampling distribution is not assumed to be normal.
    * **Metric:** The **median** `vote_share_swing`.
    * **Method:** A 95% confidence interval for the median is generated by taking 10,000 bootstrap samples (resampling the data with replacement) and calculating the median of each sample.
